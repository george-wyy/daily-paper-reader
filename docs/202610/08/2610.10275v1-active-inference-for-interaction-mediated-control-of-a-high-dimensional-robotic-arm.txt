Abstract. We propose interaction-mediated control via Active Infer-ence as a general architectural approach to high-dimensional control problems in Human–Computer Interaction (HCI). This architecture re-casts user interaction as the provision of evidence about a latent task ob-jective, rather than the direct specification of plant-control inputs. The mediating function is distributed between an interaction broker, which selects informative user queries and performs Bayesian inference over user preferences, and an Active Inference controller, which autonomously plans and acts under the resulting preference information to control the plant. This division of labour decouples the semantics of user interac-tion from those of low-level plant control. We instantiate the architec-ture in a simulated, multi-link robotic arm to perform a simultaneous whole-arm target-coverage task. A simulated user communicates exclu-sively through a clutch-style binary evaluative channel, without specify-ing joint-torque commands. Across increasing arm dimensionalities, the architecture achieves successful interaction-mediated control, although task success is lower than when the controller receives the true target preferences directly. Exact target-subset identification also remains im-perfect, highlighting the distinction between preference inference and successful task completion. The experimental findings provide an initial computational demonstration of the proposed architecture under con-trolled, matched-model assumptions and motivate its further investiga-tion in broader HCI applications. 

Keywords: Active Inference · Human–Computer Interaction · high– dimensional Control · Interactive Robotics 

## 1 Introduction 

Users of interactive, computational systems often express their intentions and preferences at a higher semantic level than the space of controls required to re-alise them. “Tie my shoelaces” is a concise, abstract imperative, over and above all the constituent fine motor control commands that will constitute its execu-tion. In consequence, there is a fundamental distinction between the specifica-tion of what a system should achieve and the determination of how it should arXiv:2610.10275v1 [cs.HC] 7 Oct 2026 2 F. C. Paterson et al. 

be achieved. Requiring users to directly interface with the low level controls can become burdensome when those controls involve many dynamically coupled variables or if they cannot be exposed through a tractable interface compatible with the limited bandwidth of human interaction [12]. This problem is espe-cially prominent in robotic, assistive, and shared-autonomy settings, where low-bandwidth user input must be reconciled with comparatively high-dimensional control [7,4,3]. We address this problem through interaction-mediated control via Active Inference , in which user input communicates evidence about their latent, task-relevant preferences, as opposed to direct control. A mediating architecture infers the user’s preferences from interaction and communicates the resulting user-preference information to an autonomous, active inference controller in di-rect control of the plant. We propose an explicit division of labour within the mediating system, separating an Interaction Broker Agent (IBA), responsible for querying and inferring user preferences, from a Low–Level Control Agent (LLCA), responsible for plant control, under the resulting preference informa-tion. Section 2 develops this architectural proposal. Our principal contribution is the formal specification and computational re-alisation of this interaction-mediated architecture. We instantiate our approach in a simulated multi-link robotic arm and evaluate whether a clutch-style, bi-nary evaluative user-interaction channel can convey useful information about a simulated user’s latent target objective, without requiring the user to specify joint-torque controls. The resulting experiments provide an initial demonstration of the architecture and motivate its investigation in broader HCI applications. 

## 2 Interaction-Mediated Control via Active Inference 

2.1 Mediated Interaction as a General Architectural Response 

As opposed to a direct mapping from user input into low–level plant controls, a mediating mechanism may stand between the user and the plant. We refer to this general arrangement as interaction-mediated control . The mere fact of mediation does not prescribe a particular semantics for user input. For example, user input might retain the semantics of a control command, expressed through a lower-dimensional latent control space afforded by the mediating mechanism. An alternative semantics is to treat user input as evaluative evidence about whether the mediator’s proposed behaviour accords with the user’s task objective. This alternative decouples the semantics of user interaction from the semantics of Low–Level plant actuation. We adopt this latter semantics: user interaction pro-vides evidence about the user’s latent task-preference, while the mediating sys-tem determines the plant actions through which the inferred objective may be realised. 

2.2 Active Inference as an Interaction-Mediating Mechanism 

We propose Active Inference (AIF) as a framework for instantiating this evalua-tive form of interaction-mediated control [2]. Rather than treating user input as Active Inference for Interaction-Mediated Control 3

a direct control command, an Active-Inference mediator maintains probabilistic beliefs about latent user preferences. User-facing queries serve the epistemic role of soliciting user-feedback expected to reduce uncertainty about user-preferences. The resulting preference information can subsequently guide pragmatic, plant-facing actions, directed towards the realisation of the inferred objective. This provides a common, inferential perspective on the related issues of learning what 

the user wants in addition to how these preferences can be realised. Recent work on Active Inference for HCI has similarly identified an online “transduction” configuration in which an Active Inference interface lies between a user and an existing system and mediates information flow between them [8]. We refer to the resulting arrangement as interaction-mediated control via Active Inference . Active Inference has also been investigated for robotic body perception and reaching [9], and for robot-arm torque control [6]. Our focus here is on coupling autonomous plant control to preference information inferred through user interaction. Previous work has also employed Active Inference to model mouse pointing and clicking in continuous state, observation, and action spaces [5]. The present work instead investigates a mediated architecture in which a simulated user’s latent objective is inferred from evaluative interaction before autonomous plant control. 

2.3 Decomposing the AIF-Mediator: Inferential Division of Labour 

The proposed mediating function exists to solve two distinct problems: infer-ence over what the user wants and determining how the inferred objective can be realised through autonomous plant control. We make this division explicit by decomposing the mediating function into an Interaction Broker Agent (IBA) and Low–Level Control Agent (LLCA). The IBA queries the user and performs exact Bayesian inference over latent user preferences. The LLCA performs au-tonomous plant control under the resulting posterior-marginal preference infor-mation supplied by the IBA. We simulate the user side of the interaction and refer to this entity as the Human/User Agent (HUA) which has private access to the task objective and provides evaluative feedback to the IBA. The result-ing architecture is deliberately heterogeneous: its components perform distinct, local computations and communicate through restricted interfaces rather than forming a single, monolithic inference-and-control mechanism.

## 4 Experimental Design 

4.1 Experimental Question and Task 

We evaluate the interaction-mediated control architecture in silico using the MuJoCo-based generative process implemented via dm_control [11]. We use the simultaneous whole-arm coverage task defined in Section 3, with K = 4 circular workspace objects arranged in a 2 × 2 grid and exactly two target objects per episode. All six possible two-object target subset hypotheses are evaluated. The principal experimental variable is the arm’s dimensionality (number of links: N ,from 3 to 6). For each N , all six target subsets are evaluated over 24 repeated episodes, yielding 144 episodes per arm dimensionality and 576 episodes per experimental condition. 4.2 Matched Experimental Conditions 

An LLCA-only parameter search selected one configuration per arm dimen-sionality. For N = 3 , 4, 5, 6, the respective (M, H, AR) settings were (64 , 2, 25) ,

(64 , 2, 15) , (64 , 4, 15) , and (32 , 4, 15) , where M is the number of candidate plans, 

H the temporal depth per candidate plan, and AR the action-repeat factor. The isotropic process-noise covariance was fixed at Q = 10 −3I2N for all arm dimen-sionalities.. These settings are held fixed across both experimental conditions. In the direct-preference reference condition, the HUA–IBA interaction loop is disabled and the true object-wise target preference is directly supplied to the LLCA. This condition assesses controller task competence under direct access to the objective. The interaction-mediated condition is the primary experimental condition. In this setting, the HUA alone has direct access to the true target subset z∗. The IBA infers target preferences from binary HUA feedback and communicates posterior-marginal object preferences to the LLCA, as specified in Section 3. The LLCA receives no direct target information and independently plans under the communicated marginals. Task and controller settings are oth-erwise matched. The simulated HUA uses the same pool-contextual response-model family as the IBA: this is a controlled matched-model comparison, not an evaluation of real human behaviour or user-model mismatch.

## 6 Discussion and Conclusion 

Interaction-mediated control succeeded in 423 /576 episodes ( 73 .4% ), compared with 564 /576 (97 .9% ) under direct preference access. This difference plausibly reflects the additional challenge of inferring the task objective, although the experiment does not isolate the contributions of inference uncertainty, query selection, and control. The architecture achieved successful control across all tested arm dimensionalities despite imperfect target-subset identification. Since the LLCA acts on posterior-marginal object preferences rather than a MAP target hypothesis, exact preference identification and task completion constitute distinct experimental outcomes. Our present implementation employs a simulated user whose feedback pro-cess matches the response-model family assumed by the mediator.
