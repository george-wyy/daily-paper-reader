## ABSTRACT 

We often plan ambitiously yet act habitually and wonder, in retrospect, whether we would have planned differently had we known what we would actually do. Hind-sight offers a valuable perspective on past decisions, although we often wish we could have simulated hindsight at the moment of choosing. If a system could gen-erate plausible trajectories from one’s personal history, such previews might help people formulate more realistic plans and make better informed decisions. We introduce NextMe-800, an approximately 800-hour first-person dataset from one volunteer over 126 days with 1 Hz images, gaze, and audio, captioned at five hier-archical abstraction levels from atomic actions to major activities. We formulate personalized action anticipation as open-vocabulary K-step sequence prediction and construct NextAct, a 1,500-point benchmark combining NextMe-800 with the multi-person EgoLife dataset (Yang et al., 2025). Using an embedding-based soft edit distance as the metric, we evaluate how well different models can anticipate personal behavior across abstraction levels and prediction horizons. NextMe-800 and NextAct provide a months-long resource and evaluation framework for study-ing how far ahead personal behavior can be anticipated from egocentric observa-tion.

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

“Are these the shadows of the things that Will be, or are they shadows of things that May be, only?” 

Charles Dickens, A Christmas Carol (1843, Stave IV) We often make ambitious plans but struggle to anticipate how our future selves will actually behave. Initial enthusiasm for learning the piano or pursuing a research project may give way to familiar rou-tines. Hindsight helps us re-evaluate these decisions, but we wish we could simulate that perspective at the moment of choosing. If a system could preview plausible behavioral trajectories generated from one’s personal history, it might help people formulate more realistic plans. For example, it could tell someone in the morning, “Based on your past patterns, you will likely start work this afternoon, get distracted by your phone, and only resume in the evening.” Indeed, vivid encounters with future selves have been shown to reshape financial decisions (Hershfield et al., 2011), and re-cent AI-generated digital twins that simulate life paths significantly strengthen future self-continuity and expand human choice (Albrecht et al., 2026). Making such naturalistic previews reliable re-quires sustained, detailed observations of personal routines and ways to assess whether predictions match what actually happens, from immediate actions to broader activities. This raises the question: 

what data and evaluation framework are needed to support reliable, open-vocabulary prediction of a person’s future behavior from months of egocentric experience? 

Continuous egocentric recording is a natural medium for addressing these questions: it captures what a person actually does, hour by hour, with rich contextual detail. This modality has attracted growing research interest for augmenting personal memory through always-on assistants that remind the wearer of forgotten items or past events (Grauman et al., 2022), and for providing demonstra-tions for robot learning (Kareer et al., 2025). We argue that anticipating a person’s future behavior, and thereby helping them reflect on their likely choices before committing, is another promising application. Dense observation helps capture brief actions, interruptions, and transitions that sparse snapshots may miss. Following the same person over months allows us to observe how these be-haviors recur across contexts, including less frequent activities and changes in routine. However, existing resources address different aspects of this problem, providing broad egocentric diversity and an action-anticipation benchmark (Grauman et al., 2022), rich multi-person, multi-day observa-tions (Yang et al., 2025; Rossetto et al., 2025), personal memory evaluation across weeks and months (Chen et al., 2026), and personalized next-action prediction from digital interactions (Shaikh et al., 2026). To support behavioral forecasting, we need resources that combine dense sampling and extended temporal coverage with sufficient visual resolution to capture both physical and digital activities. Recording gaps must also be explicitly documented, and behavior represented at multiple levels of abstraction, from immediate actions to broader activities. Such temporal depth within one individual complements diversity across individuals by enabling repeated observations of how the same person behaves across situations and over time. We introduce NextMe-800, an approximately 800-hour first-person collection across 126 days. The record covers study, work, meals, travel, exercise, social interaction, and leisure. High-resolution RGB supports descriptions of screen-mediated actions, while gaze and audio provide complemen-tary evidence. A de-identification and captioning pipeline converts the recordings into timestamped descriptions, and a reference-preserving hierarchy represents immediate actions through major ac-tivities. In addition, NextAct combines fixed evaluation points from this collection and EgoLife (Yang et al., 2025). Its prediction target is the next K recorded events at a specified abstraction level, conditioned on the target recording windows; unobserved gaps are not treated as known be-havior. Evaluation is central yet more challenging for open-vocabulary, naturalistic events: exact string matching rejects valid paraphrases, whereas broad semantic similarity can reward the correct scene but the wrong action. We therefore validate automatic scorers against human rankings before using semantic similarity as the substitution cost in edit distance. Our contributions are threefold. First, we provide a months-long egocentric resource with an ex-plicit account of temporal coverage, annotation, and privacy preservation. Second, we formulate multi-resolution, open-vocabulary anticipation and document an embedding-based sequence met-2Preprint. Under review. ric, including human-agreement and score-distribution diagnostics. Third, we benchmark repetition, frontier language models, and a retrieval-augmented agent on NextAct, and analyze how predictabil-ity varies with abstraction level and prediction horizon. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Long-horizon action anticipation. Action anticipation has progressed from next-action classifi-cation to forecasting future activities and their timing (Furnari & Farinella, 2021; Abu Farha et al., 2018). A common formulation predicts sequences of verb–noun pairs from a fixed taxonomy, eval-uated using edit distance (Grauman et al., 2022). Recent approaches combine visual descriptions with language modeling and goal inference while retaining this closed-vocabulary formulation (Kim et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024). Personalized, open-vocabulary prediction has also been explored using one month of phone-screen actions from 20 users, although these observations are limited to digital interactions (Shaikh et al., 2026). Our dataset provides a much longer record of every-day physical and digital activities. Its five abstraction level of behavioral hierarchy supports open-vocabulary anticipation and enables studying whether weeks or months of personal history improve predictions beyond the immediate context. 

Longitudinal egocentric datasets. Longitudinal egocentric recordings range from nine months of sparse outdoor frames (Singh et al., 2016) to hundreds of hours of child-perspective video (Sul-livan et al., 2021) for developmental research. Lifelog retrieval benchmarks typically emphasize still images sampled every 30 seconds (Gurrin et al., 2021; Tran et al., 2026). Dense multi-person recordings cover up to one week (Yang et al., 2025; Rossetto et al., 2025), while personal mem-ory benchmarks extend across months with sparser sampling (Chen et al., 2026). Complementary benchmarks support goal-based understanding of procedural activities and planning from egocentric video (Song et al., 2023; Qiu et al., 2026). Our dataset combines temporal depth with 1 Hz images, gaze, and audio. The resulting abstraction levels span atomic actions to major activities without a prescribed taxonomy, supporting open-vocabulary prediction of how everyday behavior actually unfolds. 

Ultralong video understanding and behavior simulation. Structured multimodal memory sup-ports retrieval and question answering over recordings spanning hours to weeks (Yeo et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026). Extending these systems to behavior forecasting requires restricting retrieval to evidence available before the prediction point. LLMs coupled with memory and environment mod-ules can simulate interactive behavior and emergent social dynamics (Park et al., 2023; Vezhnevets et al., 2023). However, plausible simulations do not by themselves demonstrate accurate prediction of an individual’s future actions. Our longitudinal dataset enables direct evaluation against observed future behavior, using an embedding-based metric normalized against random baselines.

## VIDEO LOOKS LIKE 

Collection and design choices. To capture the full detail of one person’s daily behavior as con-tinuously as possible, NextMe-800 records one volunteer wearing Project Aria glasses (Engel et al., 2023) across 126 calendar days, producing 795.5 hours of first-person video, an average of 6.3 hours per day. RGB is sampled at 1 Hz at 2880 × 2880 resolution, accompanied by continuous gaze and audio streams, totaling 8.5 TB . There are some considerations for selecting such parameters: 1 Hz sampling also balances behav-ioral coverage against cost: most vision-language models downsample to roughly 1 fps for video understanding, and higher frame rates bring diminishing informational returns for action-level de-scription. 2880 × 2880 resolution ensures that text on phone and laptop screens remains legible, preserving the digital half of modern daily behavior. Gaze tracking provides continuous attention information that disambiguates what the wearer is focusing on when multiple objects are in view. Recording spans from shortly after waking until bedtime, with intermittent pauses for charging or incompatible activities; each recorded day therefore comprises multiple disjoint sessions (4.9 on average across 528 total sessions), and 18 of the 126 calendar days contain no recording at all. What activities does the collection cover? The recording broadly covers both habitual routines and long-tail events (see Figure 1). Recurring activities include academic research, coding, meals, dating, sports, and guitar practice. Long-tail sequences range from boarding flights, watching the sunset from a ferry, and exploring karst landscapes to playing squash, observing dolphins and beluga whales, and interacting with robotic dogs. Together, these span diverse physical environments, social contexts, cognitive tasks, and personal interests, revealing how one individual’s routines and deviations evolve over months. 

Why this temporal scale? Table 1 compares longitudinal recording statistics. Ego4D provides broad diversity across 931 participants but averages only 3.9 hours per person, precluding longitu-dinal study of any individual. EgoLife achieves dense waking-hour coverage (39.6%) comparable to ours but spans only 7 days (Yang et al., 2025). KrishnaCam follows one person for nine months yet captures only sparse outdoor frames. LongNAP records 28 days of screen interactions for 20 users but is limited to the digital domain. NTCIR-18/LSC achieves the longest span (547 days) and highest nominal coverage but samples one still image every 30 seconds without gaze or au-dio. NextMe-800 combines months-long temporal depth with 1 Hz video, gaze, and audio for a single individual, achieving 39.5% awake-time coverage—recorded hours divided by waking hours assuming 16 h awake per day—across 126 calendar days (see Figure 2). 

Privacy-aware processing. The de-identification pipeline prioritizes preserving behavioral in-formation while removing direct identifiers. Detected faces are masked via CenterFace (Xu et al., 2020); on-screen sensitive text (credentials, phone numbers, addresses) is located by PP-OCR (Zhang et al., 2026) and classified by the OpenAI Privacy Filter model (de Bourcy et al., 2026), with only identified sensitive spans masked—entertainment content, work interfaces, and message text that describe behavior are retained. Speech is transcribed to identify and mute sensitive seg-ments, and voice characteristics are transformed to reduce speaker identifiability. Together, these 4Preprint. Under review. measures provide comprehensive protection across visual, textual, and audio modalities, although subtle contextual cues may persist in a months-long record. 

## 4 FROM RAW RECORDINGS TO BEHAVIORAL DATA 

Figure 3: Simplified illustration of the hierarchical caption tree, showing three of the five abstrac-tion levels. At the lowest level, every few-second interaction is preserved (e.g., waking the phone, entering a password, opening an app). The mid level absorbs such incidental phone use and tracks subtask progression within the puzzle session. The high level collapses the entire hour-long session into a single event and the prediction target becomes the next major activity. This section describes the captioning pipeline that produces atomic action descriptions and the hier-archical abstraction that organizes them into five semantic levels. 

Dense captioning. The caption pipeline processes 30-second windows. Each window consists of 1 Hz sampled frames with the gaze trajectory rendered on each image, OCR-extracted screen text, and an aligned speech transcript. Because on-screen text is often small at the native 2880 ×2880 res-olution, a cropped region around the gaze fixation point is additionally provided to the model. We use Gemini-family models (Google DeepMind, 2026a;b) as the captioning backbone. The model returns first-person action descriptions that constitute L1 events in the abstraction hierarchy, and environment descriptions that establish the scene and subsequently record state changes. To assess caption quality, four annotators independently audit 613 caption segments, with injected erroneous captions as quality controls. After excluding one low-performing annotator, 88.25% of source seg-ments receive no error flag; the remaining errors mostly concern minor perceptual details, such as left–right hand confusion, rather than the core action (see Appendix A). 

Hierarchical caption tree. Raw L1 captions are inherently noisy: a three-hour study session pro-duces thousands of atomic events including scratching one’s nose, glancing at a phone, or adjusting the air conditioner. Events at lower abstraction levels preserve more information but are noisier and less predictable, whereas events at higher levels cover the same time span with fewer autoregressive steps, reducing compounding error at the expense of detail. No single level is optimal, so the abstrac-tion hierarchy allows switching granularity to balance noise, information content, and compounding error as needed. Following the recursive captioning paradigm (Islam et al., 2024), we organize captions into a five-level hierarchical tree (L1–L5) with adjacent-level compression ratios ranging from 1.6 × to 11 ×.Starting from L1 atomic actions, an LLM recursively abstracts each level into the next by group-ing events that share a common goal using the same prompt structure at every transition (see Ap-pendix F). Rather than feature-based clustering, the model decides merge boundaries based on se-mantic goal shifts; we impose no fixed time-scale range or minimum event duration, as constraining durations causes models to rigidly segment toward the prescribed range rather than following natural event boundaries. Each event at every level carries a single action description expressing one unified objective instead of a concatenation of child actions. “Organize purchased groceries,” for example, subsumes unpacking, sorting, and storing as a single parent. ## 5 EVALUATING OPEN -V OCABULARY ACTION SEQUENCES 

How similar are a ground-truth action “I grab a bottle of cola from the fridge” and a model predic-tion “I pick up a glass of water from the table”? We evaluate a variety of scoring methods, verify each against human agreement, and select embedding cosine similarity as the atomic action metric. For multi-step evaluation, following the edit-distance formulation in long-horizon action anticipa-tion (Grauman et al., 2022), we extend this to a soft edit distance that aligns two variable-length action sequences while tolerating insertions, deletions, and partial mismatches. 

Atomic action similarity. A good evaluation metric should both agree with human judgment on which prediction is closer to the ground truth, and spread scores enough to discriminate between an-swers of different quality rather than piling them near 0 or 1. We formalize these as two diagnostics. 

Pairwise accuracy measures ranking agreement: four annotators independently rank candidates by similarity to the ground truth, ties permitted; for each strict human preference between two candi-dates, a metric is correct if it assigns the preferred one a strictly higher score. Normalized score entropy H10 measures discrimination: all scores are binned into ten equal-width intervals on [0 , 1] 

and the Shannon entropy of bin occupancy is normalized by log 10 (Shannon, 1948); low entropy indicates a metric that compresses most scores into a narrow band and cannot supply graded substi-tution costs for sequence alignment. To construct a validation set that broadly covers the similarity spectrum, we collect 350 time points, each pairing a ground-truth action with four candidates. Candidates are drawn from two sources: real predictions from multiple models (GPT-5.4, DeepSeek-V4-Flash, Qwen3.5-35B) across diverse test points spanning different participants, granularity levels, and dates; and controlled rewrites of the ground truth at varying semantic distances (i.e., paraphrases that preserve meaning and partial rewrites that alter the verb or object). Real predictions alone cluster at low similarity, so the rewrites fill the upper range to ensure the full score distribution is represented. As shown in Table 2, Qwen3 reranker and Qwen3-Embedding-8B agree best with annotators (pair-wise accuracy 0.9417 and 0.9290, respectively), ahead of LLM judges (0.8952–0.9097) and lexi-cal metrics (0.6916–0.8269), and above the 0.8619 agreement between annotators themselves (Ap-pendix B). The reranker, however, piles 69.6% of its scores at the endpoints ( H10 = 0 .6429 ; see Figure 4), whereas embeddings spread scores across the full range ( H10 = 0 .9724 ) and thus supply graded substitution costs. Embedding scoring also needs only O(K) forward passes per sequence pair, versus O(K2) LLM-judge calls. We therefore adopt Qwen3-Embedding-8B with an instruc-tion prompt emphasizing the core action verb and object (Zhang et al., 2025), and report reranker scores as a complementary check. 6Preprint. Soft edit distance. To evaluate a predicted sequence of K actions against the ground truth, we need a metric that tolerates local errors. That is, a single missing or extra action should not shift every subsequent alignment and penalize the entire trace. Following the edit-distance formulation used in long-term action anticipation (Grauman et al., 2022), we allow insertions, deletions, and substitutions. Inserting or deleting an action costs 1. Substituting a predicted action aj for a ground-truth action gi costs cij = 1 − cos( e(gi), e (aj )) , clipped to [0 , 1] : identical actions cost 0, unrelated ones cost 1, and partial matches receive graded credit. Dynamic programming finds the minimum-cost alignment Dmn between the reference sequence g1: m and the prediction a1: n, and we normalize to obtain 

S = 1 − Dmn 

max( m, n ) . (1) At K=1 , S reduces to the clipped cosine similarity. We report S normalized against a random-prediction baseline, so that 0 corresponds to chance and 1 to a perfect match (see Appendix B). 

## 6 ANTICIPATION BENCHMARK AND EMPIRICAL FINDINGS 

How well can current methods anticipate personal behavior, and where do they fail? We system-atically evaluate a progression of approaches on our NextAct benchmark (ranging from trivially repeating the last action, through in-context learning with a frontier LLM, to retrieval-augmented and agentic systems) to locate the floor and ceiling of prediction performance and diagnose what each added capability contributes. Table 3 summarizes the main results; the following paragraphs describe each method and analyze where gains come from. 

The NextAct benchmark. To establish a unified action prediction standard, we curate 1,000 eval-uation points from NextMe-800 spanning different abstraction levels and recording dates, and add another 500 points from EgoLife (Yang et al., 2025), a second source with six participants, across levels and dates. Models must predict the next K ∈ { 1, 10 } actions. Each point specifies the recorded time windows of its target events. Recordings contain gaps, and events vary from seconds to hours in duration; without these windows, a prediction could cover a longer, shorter, or gap-spanning period than the reference, making alignment with the ground truth unfair across points. As human behavior is inherently stochastic, we allow each predictor three candidate trajectories and score only the best one, using the normalized embedding-based soft edit distance from §5. 

Context scaling test. Can a model anticipate our behavior more accurately if it sees more of our history in context? We increase the context exponentially from 1 to 3,000 most recent events and measure the resulting score with Gemini 3.7 Flash and DeepSeek-v4-flash (see Figure 5). Fine-grained (or lower abstraction) levels saturate quickly and then decline: predicting the next atomic action typically needs only the preceding few minutes, and longer inputs dilute the relevant evidence, consistent with the degradation of long-context language models as input length grows (Modarressi et al., 2025). Coarse-grained (or higher abstraction) levels benefit from longer history but still sat-urate rather than continuing to scale, suggesting that in-context learning alone cannot exploit the full potential of a months-long personal record. A 50-event context typically lies in or near the best-performing range, so all subsequent in-context experiments use 50 events by default. 

How well do different predictors perform? Human behavior exhibits strong short-term conti-nuity: a person studying is likely still studying seconds later (Miller et al., 2019). Even trivially repeating the last K observed actions captures this continuity and scores 0.19 at K=1 and 0.14 at 

K=10 , serving as the prediction floor. A stronger approach leverages the commonsense priors of LLMs through in-context learning. We prompt LLMs with the 50 most recent events in context, following the context-scaling results above. We evaluate frontier models from four families (i.e., DeepSeek-v4-flash, Claude Opus 4.6, GPT-5.6-sol, and Gemini 3.1 Pro) and two smaller models, Qwen3.5-4B and Gemma4-12B-it (Appendix E), each returning three candidate trajectories. We additionally test Codex + DeepSeek, a retrieval-augmented system that uses file-browsing tools to autonomously access earlier same-person history beyond the 50-event window. At K=1 , the frontier-model predictors cluster in the 0.32–0.34 range (see Table 3), roughly 0.14 above simple repetition of the most recent event. Gemini leads at 0.34 and Opus trails at 0.32. These closely grouped scores may suggest that frontier models tend to predict from similar behavioral dis-tributions, with no clear performance separation in this evaluation. Which abstraction level is more predictable? Figure 6 plots the mean over frontier-model pre-dictors across abstraction levels. For one-step prediction ( K=1 ), scores rise from L1–L2 to a peak around L3–L4 (mean event duration ∼7.5 and ∼24 minutes), then decline slightly at L5. Atomic L1–L2 descriptions carry substantial noise and randomness, which makes the next action prediction challenging; as granularity increases, the caption hierarchy filters out these incidental details, im-proving next-action predictability. Beyond L4, however, each event spans such a long period that the activities it connects become less continuous, and prediction difficulty rises again. For longer horizons ( K=10 ), the inverted-U shape is more pronounced: scores peak at L3 and drop sharply at L4–L5. Ten L1 events cover on average 15 seconds of behavior, while ten L5 events span 5.8 hours. The curve reflects a tradeoff between noise reduction from abstraction and increasing temporal dis-continuity at coarser scales. 

## 7 DISCUSSION 

To study how to predict strongly personalized future behavior, we introduce NextMe-800, a natu-ralistic dataset of dense first-person recordings spanning four months of one person’s daily life. By capturing everyday physical and digital activities as they unfold, the dataset provides a sustained record for studying how personal routines recur, vary across contexts, and change over time. We construct a five-level hierarchical caption tree that represents behavior at multiple levels of abstrac-tion, extend soft edit distance to open-vocabulary multi-step evaluation, and benchmark predictors ranging from repeat-last baselines to frontier LLMs and retrieval-augmented agentic systems on the 1,500-point NextAct evaluation set. Models achieve modest scores across the board, and perfor-mance consistently declines as the prediction horizon lengthens. Strongly personalized anticipation remains challenging even for frontier models. The dataset and evaluation framework provide a foundation for studying these challenges, taking a step toward personalized prediction systems and behavioral simulation, with the longer-term goal of helping people make better-informed decisions about their own futures. 9Preprint.
