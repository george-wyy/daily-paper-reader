Abstract 

Gaze estimation under natural head-eye motion underpins applications from driver monitoring to human-computer interaction. Single-frame methods predict each frame independently, so consecutive outputs fluctuate as jitter. Multi-frame methods reduce this, but they learn motion implicitly inside appearance features, so the gaze trajectory is never an explicit variable. We propose EyeTAG (Eye T rajectory-Aware Gaze Esti-mation), a causal multi-frame framework built around an explicit first-order gaze prior: at each step it differentiates its own recent predictions and feeds the resulting trajectory back as a compact kinematic token. Because differencing is translation-invariant in gaze space, this token carries subject-invariant motion rather than personal gaze offsets. Face and eye streams supply visual evidence, fused by cross-attention and a causal Trans-former decoder. EyeTAG reduces the mean angular error by about 1.0° on Gaze360 and performs on par with the strongest baseline on EVE (2.56° vs. 2.58°). Within-model ablations, which keep the encoder and the rest of the architecture fixed and vary only the gaze history, show that the differential formulation, rather than temporal context alone, removes the systematic saccade bias that persists even with an absolute gaze-history prior. Our code is available at https://github.com/peter8366/EyeTAG .

# 1 Introduction 

Gaze estimation is the task of predicting where a person is looking, and it is fundamental for understanding human attention and for enabling natural human-computer interaction. It is used in many applications, such as driver monitoring [26, 27], behavioral and cognitive anal-ysis [28], and gaze-driven interfaces for extended reality [20, 25]. Despite steady progress, achieving robust performance in unconstrained real-world conditions is still challenging, be-cause the head pose, eye appearance, occlusion, and illumination vary widely. Early gaze-estimation methods relied on geometric modeling of the eyeball and corneal reflections. These methods are accurate in controlled environments, but they require user-specific calibration and are sensitive to occlusion and illumination. In contrast, appearance-

Most appearance-based models predict gaze from a single image, so consecutive predic-tions are statistically independent and fluctuate as unrealistic jitter or physically implausible outliers during rapid head-eye movements. Temporal continuity itself is not a new concern: multi-frame methods have addressed it with recurrent layers [18], temporal attention [14], and sequence models over per-frame features [17]. However, these methods represent mo-tion only implicitly , inside high-dimensional appearance features — the gaze trajectory is never carried as an explicit variable, and the model must rediscover it at every step. This temporal continuity means that, for stable estimation, it is important to condition the current prediction on the recent gaze kinematics, that is, on the sequence of past differentials rather than on appearance alone. To this end, we propose EyeTAG (Eye T rajectory-Aware Gaze Estimation), a causal multi-frame framework whose defining component is an explicit, autoregressive gaze prior. Unlike previous temporal methods, which learn motion implicitly from high-dimensional appearance features, EyeTAG takes its own recent predictions, converts them into first-order differences, and feeds this differential trajectory back into the model as (i) a kinematic prior encoded by a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP). The differencing operator is translation-invariant in gaze space, so a constant offset applied to the whole trajectory leaves the prior unchanged; the prior therefore carries the motion patterns that are shared across subjects and discards subject-specific resting-gaze offsets. To ground this prior in reliable visual evidence, EyeTAG additionally encodes (ii) global facial context with a ResNet-50 and (iii) fine-grained ocular cues with a ResNet-18, and a fusion module based on cross-attention and a causal Transformer decoder integrates the three streams. Both the visual window and the prediction history contain only past and current information, so the whole model is causal and can be run in a streaming setting. # 2 Related Works 

## 2.1 Gaze Estimation 

Geometric-based Methods. Early gaze-estimation approaches modeled the 3D geometry of the eyeball and corneal reflections [11, 12]. These methods can achieve high accuracy in controlled environments, but they usually require user-specific calibration and are sensitive to illumination and occlusion. They provide explicit physical constraints for gaze estimation, but because they depend on specialized hardware and constrained acquisition conditions, they are difficult to apply in real-world scenarios. 

Appearance-based Methods. With the development of deep learning, appearance-based methods have become the preferred approach [1, 5, 6, 18, 19, 32]. These methods learn a di-rect mapping from visual appearance to the 3D gaze direction without geometric calibration. In addition, large-scale in-the-wild datasets such as GazeCapture [19] and Gaze360 [18] have allowed these data-driven methods to generalize across diverse subjects and scenes. Cheng et al . [6] proposed an asymmetric regression that uses facial appearance and improves accuracy in single-frame settings. More recently, Abdelrahman et al . [1] used a multi-branch archi-tecture that regresses the pitch and yaw angles separately for more fine-grained estimation. Cheng and Lu [5] proposed GazeTR, which uses a hybrid CNN-Transformer architecture to capture long-range spatial dependencies from facial images. Xiao et al . [32] further im-proved appearance-based estimation using deformable sparse attention, but this method still does not perform explicit temporal reasoning. 

## 2.2 Temporal and Multi-frame Modeling 

Temporal modeling for gaze estimation has been widely explored. Kellnhofer et al . [18] used an LSTM to capture temporal continuity, Hu et al . [14] introduced temporal attention over informative frames, and Jindal et al . [17] proposed STAGE to combine inter-frame motion cues with global video dynamics. Other works model head-face-eye interactions [10] or eye-head-body coordination [23], while related approaches forecast future fixations from scene context [15, 16]. Unlike these methods, where temporal dynamics are represented implicitly through ap-pearance features or contextual cues, EyeTAG explicitly models gaze motion itself. ## 2.3 Cross-modal Fusion of Face and Eye Features 

Accurate gaze estimation requires an effective combination of global facial context and local ocular motion. The facial region provides coarse cues related to head pose, while the eye re-gion captures fine-grained pupil and iris motion. Early multi-branch architectures combined these cues by simple feature concatenation, but this approach often failed to align the two modalities when they were misregistered. Fischer et al . [9] extracted features from the left and right eyes using separate VGG-based branches and combined them with facial representations for gaze prediction. Cheng et al . [7] later proposed a coarse-to-fine adaptive network that first estimates a coarse gaze direction from the face and then refines it using a residual computed from the eye image. More recent cross-modal interaction frameworks, such as CrossGaze [4], use cross-attention to fuse face and eye features. In short, prior work either relies on appearance cues alone, which can lead to temporal jitter, or models temporal dynamics implicitly through high-dimensional appearance features. EyeTAG instead explicitly models gaze kinematics and fuses the resulting prior with multi-scale face and eye features. 

# 3 Method 

Let Xt,T = {xt−T +1, · · · , xt } denote a sequence of T consecutive frames up to and including the current frame xt ∈ R3×H×W . The goal of EyeTAG is to estimate the 3D gaze direction LEE ET AL.: EYETAG: EYE TRAJECTORY-AWARE GAZE ESTIMATION 5

ˆgt ∈ S 2 of the current frame xt . To do this, it uses both the spatial appearance and the temporal kinematic motion in the recent video context, while keeping the autoregressive consistency between consecutive predictions. As shown in Figure 2, EyeTAG consists of three core components and a lightweight prediction head: (i) Dual-Stream Visual Encoder (DSVE) , which extracts per-frame facial and ocular representations using a VGGFace2-pretrained ResNet-50 for the face stream and a shared ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18 for the eye streams, and stacks them along the tem-poral axis into token sequences; (ii) Trajectory-Aware Kinematic Encoder (TAKE) , which models gaze dynamics by encoding differential gaze trajectories ∆ ˆG into a compact motion token through a lightweight MLP; and (iii) Cross-Modal Causal Attention Fusion (CCAF) ,which integrates the facial, ocular, and kinematic representations using cross-modal atten-tion and a causal Transformer decoder. A lightweight Gaze Vector Predictor (GVP) then regresses the final 3D gaze vector ˆ gt from this fused representation.

# 4 Experiments 

## 4.1 Dataset 

We conduct experiments on the Gaze360 [18] and EVE [24] datasets. Gaze360 was captured from 238 participants under natural conditions in seven environments at 30 Hz, and it covers a wide head-pose range of ±180 ◦ yaw and ±90 ◦ pitch. Following the official split proto-col [18], we use 84,902 training, 11,318 validation, and 16,031 test annotated frames, which are drawn from 321, 85, and 68 clips, respectively, as summarized in Table 1. The training, validation, and test splits are subject-disjoint, so that there is no identity leakage. Following the original Gaze360 convention, we report results on three head-pose subsets defined by the absolute yaw of the target frame: All (|yaw | ≤ 180 ◦), Semi-Front (|yaw | ≤ 90 ◦), and Front 

(|yaw | ≤ 20 ◦); these subsets are visualized in Sec. S1 of the supplementary material. We also evaluate EyeTAG on the EVE dataset [24]. EVE is a large-scale video-based gaze estimation benchmark that was collected using synchronized webcam videos, screen recordings, and eye-tracking devices during natural desktop interaction scenarios. Because EVE contains temporally continuous gaze sequences, it is an appropriate benchmark for evaluating temporal autoregressive gaze estimation methods. Following the official protocol, we train on the official training split and evaluate on the official validation split, because the test annotations are not publicly available. We use only the webcam_c camera stream, which corresponds to the frontal webcam view. The split-level statistics of both datasets are summarized in Table 1.

## 4.3 Experimental Results 

Table 2 summarizes the quantitative performance on the Gaze360 [18] and EVE [24] bench-marks. We compare EyeTAG with two groups of baselines: single-frame methods (L2CS-Net [1], GazeTR-Hybrid [5], and CrossGaze [4]) and multi-frame methods (Gaze360 [18] and STAGE [17]). All baseline numbers are obtained from our re-training under the same evaluation protocol. STAGE is evaluated in the same calibration-free setting as EyeTAG, although it was originally designed for personalized gaze estimation; this difference likely contributes to its lower Gaze360 score. We also report the (c) model complexity, namely the average inference time and the computational cost (GFLOPs), measured on a single NVIDIA RTX A6000 (FP32, batch size 1). On Gaze360, EyeTAG consistently achieves the lowest MAE on all head-pose subsets, which shows that it is effective for both frontal and large-pose variations. Specifically, Eye-TAG achieves 9.29° on the full-range subset, 9.12° on the semi-frontal subset, and 8.08° on the frontal subset, and it improves the MAE by about 1.0° over the strongest baseline. # 6 Conclusion 

In this work, we present EyeTAG, a trajectory-aware multi-frame gaze estimation framework that addresses the temporal instability of frame-based approaches. Unlike appearance-only methods that predict gaze independently for each frame, EyeTAG explicitly models gaze kinematics through differential gaze trajectories, so that the prediction becomes temporally coherent and stable under unconstrained head-eye motion. It integrates multi-scale appear-ance cues from the face and eye regions with a trajectory-aware kinematic prior learned from the differential gaze dynamics ∆ ˆG, using cross-modal attention fusion and causal temporal aggregation. On Gaze360, EyeTAG clearly reduces angular error, and on EVE it remains on par with the strongest multi-frame baseline. It also keeps fixation jitter low and reduces velocity bias during rapid transitions, and within-model ablations attribute this near-zero saccade bias to the differential prior rather than to temporal context alone. 

Limitations. The current framework only estimates the individual gaze direction, and it does not use scene context. EyeTAG models the temporal dynamics of where a person is looking, but it does not reason about what the person is looking at, or about how multiple people’s gaze patterns interact in a shared environment. Real-time performance is verified only on a desktop GPU, so embedded deployment remains future work. Bridging this gap between low-level gaze estimation and higher-level attention understanding is an important direction for future research. 

Future Work. We plan to extend this framework to multi-person and scene-aware gaze understanding, such as social gaze interactions and contextual intent, moving toward next-generation systems for complex human attention. LEE ET AL.: EYETAG: EYE TRAJECTORY-AWARE GAZE ESTIMATION 15

# S4 Effect of Scheduled Sampling 

During training, the gaze history ˆ Gt−1,T −1 fed to the TAKE module can be constructed from the ground-truth gaze (teacher forcing) or from the model’s own past predictions (autore-gressive). To reduce the train-inference mismatch between these two strategies, we use a scheduled sampling strategy [2] that linearly anneals the teacher-forcing ratio α from 1.0 to 0.0 over epochs 1-5. As summarized in Algorithm S1, after each epoch the current model runs inference on the entire training set, and the resulting predictions are stored in the cache 

C for use in the next epoch. At inference, the model processes frames sequentially, append-ing each prediction to C online. When fewer than T −1 past predictions are available, the cache is padded by repeating the first prediction ˆ g1.To validate this design choice, we compare three strategies for constructing the gaze his-tory while keeping all the other components fixed: (a) teacher forcing only (α = 1 through-out), (b) fully autoregressive (α = 0 throughout), and (c) our scheduled sampling (α annealed from 1.0 to 0.0). # S5 Effect of Window Length with Normal Gaze Prior 

In the main paper (Sec. 5.3), we sweep the temporal window length T under our proposed kinematic gaze prior ( w/ ∆ ˆG). Here we repeat the same sweep with the normal gaze prior (w/ ˆ G), which serves as a controlled baseline that separates the role of temporal context from the gaze-history representation. All experiments use the same architecture as our model (a VGGFace2-pretrained ResNet-50 face encoder, a shared ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18 eye encoder, unidirectional cross-attention fusion, and a causal Transformer decoder), and we vary only T .As reported in Table S4, the MAE decreases steadily from T =4 to T =32, which con-firms that a longer temporal context provides useful information for gaze estimation. How-ever, the accuracy starts to plateau and even degrade slightly beyond T =32. For example, the Semi-Front error rises from 9.46° at T =32 to 9.65° at T =48 and 9.91° at T =64. This saturation comes from the scale of the normal representation. As T grows, the range of abso-lute pitch–yaw values expands together with the subject-specific bias, so it becomes harder for the model to extract consistent motion patterns from longer sequences. This motivates the kinematic gaze prior ∆ ˆG in the main paper (Sec. 5.2). The differencing removes subject-dependent gaze offsets, so the accuracy keeps improving at larger T .
